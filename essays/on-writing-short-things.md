I keep coming back to short essays.

Not because I can't write long ones. I can. The long form has its uses — a sustained argument, a slow build, the feeling of a room you settle into. But the long form rewards the writer, not the reader. It lets you hide. It lets you write past the point where the idea was finished and into the territory where you're just proving you can keep going.

A short essay has no such comfort. If the idea is finished in 400 words, you stop at 400. The form is the discipline.

## What the form asks

A short essay asks three things of you:

- **Have one idea.** Not a topic — an idea. A topic is a region; an idea is a place you stand in the region. You can stand in many places, but only one at a time.
- **Defend it gently.** Argue without winning. The moment you start trying to convince someone, you've lost the shape. You're writing a debate, not an essay.
- **Stop when it's done.** This is the hardest one. Most essays — mine included — would be better at 80% of their length.

## Why it works

Short essays scale. They respect the reader's afternoon. They travel well. A friend can send one in a message. You can read one before a meeting. You can read it twice in a row, which is the highest compliment I have for any piece of writing.

And here's the secret: a short essay is harder than a long one. Every sentence has to carry weight. There is no filler because there is no room for filler. You learn to cut. You learn to cut again. Eventually you learn to write things that don't need cutting.

That's the craft. Not the sentence — the stop.
